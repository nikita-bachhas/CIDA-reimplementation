Earlier research solves only half of this. [click] Domain adaptation copes with the new look, but it needs the old photos and lumps all new things into one unknown pile. So even its best method gets only 28.6 percent on new things. [click] Class-incremental learning adds new names, but most of it ignores the new look and needs old photos or many labels. [click] CIDA is the only approach that ticks all three boxes, and it reaches 70.8 percent with one photo per new thing.

Small errors start early and then snowball. [click] Before any learning, the bridge only roughly passes features through, so 52 percent of known images already land on the new side. [click] Next, the calibration still fits the city, so known images sit closer to the new-thing magnets. Only 37.5 to 43.9 percent of the first labels are right. [click] Then it keeps training on its own wrong labels, until 57 to 60 percent of known images are called new. [click] So our fixes target the points where the errors start.

From SPEC.md, section “Our choices”. Rows 2 and 3 compare the paper's version of one part against our full configuration.

We made four fixes. [click] First, we recalibrate on the new photos, known as AdaBN. Known images wrongly labelled new drop from 74 to 78 percent, down to 14 to 21 percent. [click] Second, we build the bridge to start as an exact pass-through, which lifts accuracy from 44.8 to 71.9 on MNIST to USPS. [click] Third, the model learns only from its most confident 20 percent of labels, which lifts accuracy from 55.1 to 71.9. [click] Fourth, for office photos we keep the pretrained image network fixed. Retraining it on approximately 900 photos dropped the new-thing accuracy of its features from 72.4 to 33.1.

Some of the paper's rules do not hold up

How far real photos sit from their own class centre

Squared distance in units of the class spread, 100 dimensions as in our digit model. Everything right of the dashed line counts as “unusual”, which is approximately 100% of real photos.

The “unusual point” test accepts almost everything

A real photo already sits near 100, far past the cut-off of 9. Our stricter cut-off also accepted 100%.

(3σ rule in Algorithm 1, 100 to 256 dimensions)

It lists 11 new office classes in one place and 10 in another, and Algorithm 1 has a typo.

11 settings are missing or unclear

(for example epochs, batch size, data splits)
We also found rules in the paper that do not hold up. Its test for unusual points uses a cut-off of 9, but in 100 dimensions a typical real point already scores about 100. So almost everything passes, and our stricter test changed nothing either. The paper also contradicts itself on the office classes and leaves 11 settings missing or unclear.

Take-aways

What we learned, and what comes next

1

Run the paper exactly as written firstIt showed where accuracy goes, for example 43.7 against 72.5 on MNIST→USPS.

2

Treat unstated details as part of the methodRecalibrating on the new photos cut wrong “new” labels from 74 to 78% down to 14 to 21%.

3

Test every cut-off in high dimensionsA cut-off of 9 meets real photos that sit near 100.
